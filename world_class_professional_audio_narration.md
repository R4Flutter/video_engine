## 0. EDITORIAL POSITION

This is not a “retail investors defeated Wall Street” victory speech, and it is not a “naked shorts explain everything” conspiracy script.

The stronger story is more complicated:

**A failing-looking retailer became the center of an extreme collision between short sellers, retail investors, options activity, social media, market plumbing, and a financial system built around positions that could become dangerous when prices moved violently.**

The viewer should begin with a mystery, then gradually understand the mechanism, then realize that the most important part of the GameStop story is not simply who won.

It is how quickly a belief became a market event.

The script deliberately avoids presenting naked short selling as the proven cause of the squeeze. The SEC staff report found high short interest, observed short covering during parts of the rise, and concluded that positive sentiment—not buying-to-cover alone—sustained the weeks-long appreciation. The report also found spikes in failures to deliver but noted that fails can arise from both long and short sales and are therefore an imperfect measure of naked short selling. citeturn757065view3turn757065view4

## 1. VOICE PERFORMANCE RULES

Write and perform for the ear, not the page.

Use contractions. Use short sentences. Let important numbers breathe. Avoid sounding like a financial textbook. The narrator should sound like a calm investigative storyteller who knows the answer but understands that the audience does not.

**Base delivery:** 140–150 spoken words per minute.  
**Hook:** 155–165 WPM with clean articulation.  
**Technical explanation:** 130–145 WPM.  
**Major reveal:** 115–130 WPM for the sentence immediately around the reveal.  
**Reflection:** 125–140 WPM.

These are performance targets, not fixed timing rules. Final duration comes from the generated audio waveform, not estimated scene length.

Use three kinds of silence:

- **Micro pause:** 80–150 ms after a key phrase.
- **Normal pause:** 150–300 ms between ideas.
- **Dramatic pause:** 300–700 ms before a major reveal or reversal.

Never pause mechanically after every sentence. Silence is valuable because it is rare.

# CHAPTER 1 — THE GHOST IN THE SYSTEM

### SCENE 09 — THE KITCHEN TABLE
**Delivery:** direct address, conversational

**Narration:**

Imagine you're sitting at your kitchen table.

You open your brokerage account.

Maybe you have five hundred dollars.

Maybe a thousand.

You are not running a hedge fund. You do not have a Bloomberg terminal. You are not calling analysts all day.

You just have a screen, a thesis, and the ability to press buy.

Now imagine learning that the company you've been studying is being heavily shorted by professional investors who believe its future is terrible.

That changes the game.

### SCENE 10 — THE NUMBERS
**Delivery:** measured, credible

**Narration:**

GameStop's short interest had become extraordinary.

The SEC's later reconstruction found short interest reached **122.97 percent of the float in January 2021**—a level far above what was normal for other stocks.

And yes, short interest can exceed one hundred percent.

That sounds impossible until you understand the plumbing.

The same share can be lent, sold, bought, and then lent again.

One piece of stock can support multiple short positions.

The number isn't magic.

It's leverage built into the ownership chain.

### SCENE 11 — THE IMPOSSIBLE NUMBER
**Delivery:** explanatory, slow enough to understand

**Narration:**

Think about one hundred shares.

If five are sold short, short interest is five percent.

But imagine one of those shares is sold short, then bought by another investor, and later lent out again.

Now the same underlying share can be part of another short sale.

Repeat the process enough times, and reported short interest can rise above one hundred percent of the shares available.

That is what happened with GameStop.

The important insight is not that someone broke mathematics.

It is that the financial system had created a position where the bearish side could become extremely crowded.

### SCENE 12 — THE DYING COMPANY
**Delivery:** colder, documentary tone

**Narration:**

And the bears had reasons.

GameStop was a legacy retailer trapped in a market moving from physical discs toward digital downloads.

Its stores depended on foot traffic. Its competitive position had been under pressure. The basic story looked simple: the old model was dying.

Short sellers were betting on that decline.

For years, the trade made sense.

Until someone started asking a different question.

What if the market was underestimating what GameStop could become?

### SCENE 13 — THE BREAK
**Delivery:** one sentence, then pause

**Narration:**

And that's when something broke.

`[DRAMATIC PAUSE]`

Not the company.

The consensus.

### SCENE 14 — THE HIDDEN MECHANISM
**Delivery:** mysterious but factual

**Narration:**

Because markets don't move on fundamentals alone.

They move on positioning.

They move on expectations.

They move when one side of a trade suddenly discovers that its exit is crowded.

And this is where the GameStop story gets misunderstood.

The internet often compresses it into one phrase: naked short selling.

But that phrase is not the whole explanation.

### SCENE 15 — THE TERM
**Delivery:** precise, controlled reveal

**Narration:**

Naked short selling is a real market practice with a specific meaning: a seller makes a short sale without first arranging to borrow the security in time for settlement.

That can produce a failure to deliver.

And the SEC did observe spikes in GameStop failures to deliver.

But here's the important part.

A failure to deliver is not automatically proof of illegal naked short selling.

The SEC explicitly noted that fails can result from long or short sales and are therefore an imperfect proxy.

So the serious story is not, “We found the one illegal trick that caused everything.”

The serious story is that a heavily shorted stock entered a period of extreme buying pressure and unstable market mechanics.

### SCENE 16 — THE BEARS
**Delivery:** clinical

**Narration:**

Short sellers were not villains simply because they were short.

Short selling is a legitimate market function.

It can expose bad information. It can challenge inflated prices.

The risk is structural.

When you buy a stock, your loss can fall only to zero.

When you short one, there is no hard ceiling on your potential loss.

A stock can keep rising.

And every dollar higher makes the exit more painful.

### SCENE 17 — THE INSTABILITY
**Delivery:** increasingly tense

**Narration:**

Now add a rapidly rising price.

Add options activity.

Add forced risk management.

Add millions of people watching the same ticker.

Suddenly, a trade that looked rational at twenty dollars can look catastrophic at forty.

Then eighty.

Then one hundred.

The price itself becomes information.

And the information changes what everyone does next.

### SCENE 18 — THE SECRET
**Delivery:** intimate, pull the listener forward

**Narration:**

Here's the part nobody notices when they look at the chart after the fact.

The squeeze wasn't one switch being flipped.

It was a feedback loop.

Price rises.

People notice.

More people buy.

Some shorts cover.

Buying pushes the price higher.

The higher price changes the risk.

And the new risk changes behavior again.

### SCENE 19 — OUTSIDE THE ROOM
**Delivery:** wider perspective

**Narration:**

And suddenly the people watching from Reddit, Discord, YouTube, and brokerage apps were participating in the same market as firms with vastly different resources.

They did not have the same information.

They did not have the same tools.

But for the first time, they had something that could scale incredibly fast.

Coordination through attention.

Not perfect coordination.

Not a single command center.

Just thousands of people seeing the same idea at roughly the same time.

### SCENE 20 — THE PARADOX
**Delivery:** quiet, high-stakes

**Narration:**

And that created a strange paradox.

The same internet that made retail investors feel powerless against professional finance also gave them a way to move together without formally organizing.

The question was no longer whether one person could beat a hedge fund.

It was whether millions of small decisions could combine into something the traditional system was not designed to absorb.

And that is where Keith Gill enters the story.

### SCENE 27 — THE COUCH
**Delivery:** human, intimate

**Narration:**

### SCENE 29 — THE DETECTIVE WORK
**Delivery:** analytical, accelerating toward the final line

**Narration:**

Gill looked at GameStop differently because he connected pieces other investors were treating separately.

The low valuation.

The enormous short interest.

The possibility of a turnaround.

The new board direction.

And the fact that heavily shorted stocks can behave very differently when buyers suddenly appear.

The thesis was not “this stock goes up because the internet likes it.”

The deeper thesis was:

**What happens when a stock everyone expects to die suddenly has buyers who refuse to leave?**

**Narration:**

Most of the market still saw the same old GameStop.

A crowded short trade.

A possible turnaround.

A rapidly growing online audience.

And a market where every new buyer could force someone else to reconsider the position they were already holding.

By January 2021, those ingredients were no longer sitting separately.

They were colliding.

And when they did, GameStop stopped being a company story.

It became a market-structure story.

**And the next question was the one nobody could answer safely anymore:**

**How high can a stock go when the people betting against it are the ones who may have to buy it?**

# 3. RETENTION ENGINEERING BUILT INTO THE SCRIPT

The narration intentionally uses a repeating **question → partial answer → larger question** structure.

Examples:

> “Who was going to be forced to buy it next?”

Answer: short sellers may need to cover.

New question: what makes a crowded short position dangerous?

Then:

> “What if the market was underestimating what GameStop could become?”

Answer: the Cohen thesis and a possible transformation.

New question: what happens when that fundamental thesis collides with extreme positioning?

Then:

> “How high can a stock go when the people betting against it are the ones who may have to buy it?”

This structure is deliberate. Each answer should close one curiosity loop while immediately opening the next.

YouTube itself advises creators to inspect the first 30 seconds, identify top moments, and move compelling material earlier when strong moments are appearing too late. # 5. NUMBER AND TERMINOLOGY RULES

Numbers should be spoken in human language.

Say “one hundred twenty-two point nine seven percent,” not “122.97%.”

Say “January twenty-seventh,” not “01/27/21.”

Say “four hundred eighty-three dollars” when the exact number is narratively important.

Do not stack multiple statistics without interpretation. The viewer must understand why the number matters before the next number arrives.

Avoid repeating the same fact in multiple forms unless the second version adds meaning.

The most important GameStop factual anchors used here are:

- The SEC staff report found GME short interest reached **122.97% of float in January 2021**. citeturn757065view3
- The SEC report states GME's short interest reached **109.26% of shares outstanding on December 31, 2020** and explains how short interest can exceed 100% through successive lending. citeturn757065view3
- GME rose from a January 11 close of **$19.95** to **$31.40 on January 13**, then reached **$347.51 by the January 27 close**; the following day it hit an intraday high of **$483.00**. citeturn204098search48
- The SEC staff observed that short covering contributed to price increases during some discrete periods, but concluded that positive sentiment, rather than buying-to-cover alone, sustained the weeks-long appreciation. citeturn757065view3
- The SEC staff observed spikes in GameStop failures to deliver but explicitly warned that fails are an imperfect measure of naked short selling and did not find persistent fails at the individual clearing-member level. citeturn757065view4
- GameStop's January 11, 2021 filing documented the addition of Ryan Cohen and two other directors to its board. citeturn204098search7

# 6. WHAT THIS SCRIPT FIXES FROM THE ORIGINAL DRAFT

### 1. It removes false or inflated stakes.

The original line that ordinary investors with a few hundred dollars “owned companies larger than Walmart” is not a defensible description of the GameStop episode. It has been replaced with a more powerful and accurate contrast: very small initial capital could become economically significant when paired with extreme price movement.

### 2. It removes the fake 19% short-interest framing.

Nineteen percent dramatically understates the central GameStop setup. The SEC's reconstructed figure reached 122.97% of float in January 2021. citeturn757065view3

### 3. It removes the “entire stock market froze” claim.

GameStop created extraordinary volatility and market-structure stress, but saying the entire market froze is dramatic shorthand rather than a precise factual claim. The new version says the normal rules felt inadequate and later explains the actual market mechanics.

### 4. It treats naked short selling correctly.

Naked short selling is explained, but it is not used as a magic explanation for the whole event. This is essential for credibility.

### 5. It introduces the mechanism before the character worship.

Keith Gill appears only after the viewer understands why a heavily shorted stock is structurally interesting. That makes the character relevant instead of turning the documentary into fan fiction.

### 6. It makes the viewer participate mentally.

The repeated use of “Imagine…” and second-person framing is intentional. The viewer is asked to inhabit the trade before being told the conclusion.

### 7. It gives the edit room to breathe.

Not every scene is written to contain the same amount of speech. Quiet beats are intentionally short. Explanation beats are longer. Reveal beats contain room for silence.

# 7. AUDIO-TIMING CONTRACT

The narration audio is the **source of truth**.

Do not set every scene to five seconds.

Do not force a sentence into a scene because the visual was generated as a nominal five-second clip.

For each narration file:

```text
actual audio duration
        ↓
scene duration
        ↓
Remotion frames = round(duration × 30)
        ↓
visual camera/effect adapts to that duration
```

A two-second line should be a two-second scene.

A six-second explanation should be allowed to breathe for six seconds.

A one-second dramatic line should not be padded with dead silence simply to reach an arbitrary target.

For scenes with intentional silence, the silence belongs to the audio timeline and must be represented explicitly in the narration/audio asset.

Transitions must fit **inside** the neighboring scene durations rather than extending the program beyond the narration timeline.

# 8. TTS / CHATTERBOX PERFORMANCE NOTES

Use punctuation as performance notation, but do not over-punctuate.

Prefer:

> “And that's when something broke.”

Then generate the pause in the voice-performance layer rather than adding theatrical punctuation such as five periods or dozens of exclamation marks.

For major reveals, split the semantic beat:

> “Not the company.”
>
> `[pause]`
>
> “The consensus.”

The voice engine should interpret the first fragment as a setup and the second as the payoff.

Avoid all-caps inside the actual spoken script except for words that must be treated as explicit emphasis by the parser.

# 9. AUDIO RETENTION CHECKLIST

Before approving the narration:

- The first 10 seconds contain no greeting, channel introduction, or generic context.
- The first 30 seconds establish consequence and a concrete unresolved question.
- Every major explanatory section answers one question while opening another.
- At least one meaningful shift in stakes occurs roughly every 30–60 seconds.
- Numbers are immediately interpreted.
- Technical terminology is explained at first use.
- No accusation is presented as fact without evidence.
- No sentence exists only because a visual needs something to say.
- No paragraph reads like an article when spoken aloud.
- Major reveals have enough silence around them to register.
- The final line creates forward momentum into the next chapter rather than sounding like a conclusion.

# 10. SOURCING / FACT DISCIPLINE

Primary factual anchors should come from SEC filings and SEC staff research wherever possible. The SEC's January 2021 GameStop report is the central source for short interest, price/volume behavior, short covering, options dynamics, market structure, and failures to deliver. citeturn204098search45turn757065view3turn757065view4

Do not use Reddit posts, viral screenshots, anonymous tweets, or meme-era claims as evidence for a factual statement merely because the claim is famous.

For editorial purposes, **“the market,” “hedge funds,” and “retail investors” are not monolithic characters.** The narration should describe incentives and observable behavior rather than assigning one motive to every participant.

# 11. FINAL EDITORIAL STANDARD

The goal is not to make the viewer feel manipulated.

The goal is to make the viewer feel that every thirty seconds they understand the story better than they did thirty seconds earlier.

A world-class finance documentary does three things simultaneously:

**It creates tension.**  
The viewer needs to know what happens next.

**It creates understanding.**  
The viewer needs to know why it is happening.

**It creates trust.**  
The viewer needs to believe the narrator will distinguish evidence from mythology.

The GameStop story has enough real tension that the script does not need fake drama.

The short interest was real.

The price movement was real.

The retail attention was real.

The market-structure questions were real.

The mistake is turning those facts into a simpler story than the evidence supports.

The stronger documentary is the opposite.

Start with the mystery.

Explain the mechanism.

Humanize the people.

Let the numbers become frightening on their own.

And when the viewer finally thinks they understand what happened, open the next question.

That is how this 12-minute film keeps moving.
