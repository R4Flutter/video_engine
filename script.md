## 2. RETENTION-FIRST EDITORIAL RULES

- **170 wpm.** The delivery is measured; urgency comes from information, contrast, silence and visual progression, not shouting.
- **Every 20–45 seconds, create a fresh unanswered question.** Resolve one question, immediately replace it with a more important one.
- **Every 45–90 seconds, change the mechanism or entity.** Do not let a chapter become one long explanation.
- **Before every likely exit point, introduce a reason to continue.** The reason must be narrative, not a generic promise.
- **Never dump the thesis all at once.** Reveal the model progressively: gym → breakage → cancellation friction → ownership → fragmented subscriptions → cancellation maze → regulation → consumer action.
- **No fake suspense.** Every open loop must eventually pay off in this video.
- **No repeated conclusion lines.** Each return to the central idea must deepen it.
- Full beat of silence before every major dollar figure or legal outcome.
- Every 4–8 seconds: a meaningful visual state change, not necessarily a cut.
- No "guys", "let's dive in", "crazy", "insane", or generic creator language.
- Do not overuse kinetic typography. Numbers are events; ordinary sentences can live on photographs, documents and real environments.
- The same cold-open gym composition returns at 17:35 and must match closely enough to feel like one continuous thought.
- Legal claims stay framed as allegations / settlements / court rulings exactly as sourced.

### Human-retention principles applied

1. **Prediction:** let the viewer form a hypothesis before overturning it.
2. **Identification:** make the viewer recognize their own subscription behavior.
3. **Contrast:** show the obvious business model, then reveal the hidden one underneath it.
4. **Escalation:** each answer should create a more consequential question.
5. **Reversal:** the “consumer-friendly” version becomes more interesting, not less.
6. **Progressive revelation:** hold back the final mechanism until the viewer has enough evidence to infer it.
7. **Callback:** return to the empty gym so the original image changes meaning after the audience understands the system.

`[VISUAL: EMPTY PLANET FITNESS AT 4:00 A.M. Wide locked-off shot. Fluorescent hum. Machines perfectly clean. Nobody there. Hold a full 5 seconds before voice.]`

**NARRATION**

Planet Fitness ended 2025 with about twenty point eight million members.

It had two thousand eight hundred and ninety-six clubs.

That is roughly seven thousand two hundred members for every location.

`[VISUAL: Membership number → club count → 7,200 ratio. Three hard cuts. No decorative animation.]`

Now make one simple assumption.

What happens if even a small fraction of those people actually show up on the same night?

`[VISUAL: Occupancy figure appears under the membership figure. Let the mismatch sit.]`

Four percent would be about eight hundred and thirty thousand members across the chain.

Ten percent would be more than two million.

The exact nightly attendance varies, of course. But the economic point is stranger than the number.

A gym only works because almost all of the people paying for it are somewhere else.

`[VISUAL: Cut back to the empty room. One person appears on one treadmill, tiny in frame.]`

And that creates a question that sounds almost backwards.

What if the customer who never shows up is not the problem?

What if they are the ideal customer?

`[BEAT — 0.8s silence.]`

Because the company does not need to sell you a workout.

It needs to keep the payment alive.

**[RETENTION LOCK — 0:35]**

And once you see that, the gym stops being a story about fitness.

It becomes a story about forgetting.

### THE STAKE — 0:40

`[VISUAL: Phone screen. A bank statement scrolling. Small recurring charges pass without attracting attention.]`

**NARRATION**

Here's the number that makes that idea much harder to dismiss.

When researchers asked a thousand Americans to estimate what they spent on subscriptions each month, the average answer was eighty-six dollars.

Then they made people walk through the charges one by one: streaming, music, cloud storage, the gym, the app downloaded for a trip two years ago.

`[VISUAL: $86. Full black. Beat. Replace with $219.]`

The itemized figure was two hundred and nineteen.

A hundred and thirty-three dollars a month higher than the estimate.

Sixteen hundred dollars a year.

`[VISUAL: $133 grows once, then freezes. Do not animate it again.]`

And the important part is not that people are bad at arithmetic.

It's that recurring payments are unusually good at becoming background noise.

You decide once.

The software decides the next payment.

Then the next one.

Then the next one.

`[VISUAL: A $10 charge appears repeatedly down a bank statement; the cursor scrolls past it without stopping.]`

Think about the last subscription you stopped noticing.

You probably did not make a new decision every month to keep it.

You simply failed to make a new decision to end it.

That is a very different kind of transaction.

And that difference is where this story begins.

Because once one company realises you can be charged without re-asking you every month, another company can copy the mechanism.

Then another.

Then an entire economy starts treating the recurring charge as the default.

**[OPEN LOOP]**

The strange part is that this did not begin with software.

It began somewhere much more ordinary.

### ACT I — BREAKAGE — 1:35

`[VISUAL: Archival recreation — 1980s American health club. Aerobics, chrome machines, mirrored walls, VHS texture.]`

**NARRATION**

Start with the gym, because the gym makes the economics visible without hiding behind software.

A gym has a strange cost structure.

The building costs roughly the same whether one person walks in or a thousand do.

The machines cost the same.

The lease, lights, insurance and basic staffing do not suddenly double because another person walks through the door.

`[VISUAL: Cost line sits flat. Revenue line rises with each additional member.]`

So an additional member can be incredibly valuable — until that member actually uses the thing they bought.

Then they need a machine.

A shower.

Floor space.

Staff time.

The economics change the instant the customer consumes the product.

`[VISUAL: Second line labeled COST TO SERVE. Flat for a moment, then spikes at real usage.]`

So the ideal customer is not mysterious.

It is the customer who pays and does not consume much.

Industry estimates have put the share of memberships that go essentially unused at up to two-thirds. That remains an estimate, not a universal audited figure.

But the behavior behind it is obvious.

People intend to go.

Most people intended to go in January.

`[VISUAL: Google Trends-style recreation — January spike, February collapse. Repeat across several years.]`

And then the month changes.

The card still gets charged.

The person is back at work.

The treadmill is somewhere they meant to be.

`[VISUAL: Calendar advances while the same charge continues.]`

There is a word for revenue a company collects for something it does not have to deliver.

`[VISUAL: BLACK. Single word, centre frame, held.]`

**Breakage.**

`[SFX: one dry paper click. 1.5s silence.]`

The term became familiar in the gift-card business.

A card gets bought.

Part of it gets spent.

The rest goes into a drawer.

Eventually, the unused value can become revenue without another product having to be shipped.

Airlines have versions of it with expired miles.

Retailers have versions with store credit.

But a gift card breaks once.

`[VISUAL: One gift card fades out.]`

A subscription can break next month.

And the month after that.

And the month after that — until somebody actively interrupts the payment.

`[VISUAL: Calendar cards continue below frame. Same charge repeats forever.]`

That changes the value of forgetting.

For a subscription company, forgetting is not a one-time accident.

It can become recurring revenue.

**[RETENTION RESET]**

And now the next question is obvious: if that works so well, why did the older gym chains need giant contracts at all?

Because the first version of this business model had a problem.

The contract was too visible.

### BALLY — 5:35

`[VISUAL: Archival recreation — Bally Total Fitness storefront, 1990s. Cut to membership desk with contracts.]`

**NARRATION**

The first companies to industrialise this got one important thing wrong.

They made the trap too obvious.

Through the nineties, Bally was the dominant American gym chain, and its model leaned heavily on long contracts and difficult cancellation terms.

`[VISUAL: Contract. Camera glides past the signature line into dense small print.]`

Regulators investigated aspects of Bally's billing and cancellation practices in the 1990s and later. Consumers complained about unauthorised charges, short-term offers becoming long obligations, and cancellation disputes. Bally settled various matters and agreed to reforms.

`[VISUAL: Newspaper clippings, complaint forms, consumer-affairs segments. Dry treatment.]`

Now stop thinking about this as a morality story.

Think about the product-design problem.

A hard contract creates a customer who can prove they tried to leave.

That creates complaints.

Complaints create regulators.

Regulators create settlements.

`[VISUAL: BLACK. White line: THE CONTRACT WAS THE WRONG TOOL.]`

So imagine you are the next generation of gym operator.

You still want recurring revenue.

You still want predictable cash flow.

You still know a large percentage of members will stop attending.

What do you change?

You remove the thing that creates the clearest complaint.

The visible contract.

`[VISUAL: Planet Fitness storefront. Bright, cheap, unthreatening.]`

Planet Fitness will let you cancel.

And that is exactly why the model becomes more interesting.

You do not need a three-year trap if leaving simply costs an irritating afternoon.

`[VISUAL: Generic login → password reset → retention screen → confirmation. No melodramatic treatment.]`

Now add one more decision.

Price.

Charge ninety-nine dollars a month and the customer notices.

It sits on the statement like a problem.

Charge ten dollars and something different happens.

`[VISUAL: Bank statement. One $10 line item sits among dozens of similarly sized transactions.]`

Ten dollars is not free.

But cancellation has a time cost.

Find the login.

Remember the email.

Get through the retention screen.

Answer the question about why you are leaving.

Twenty minutes of irritation to recover ten dollars.

Most people do the mental calculation without writing it down.

They keep paying.

Not because they cannot afford to stop.

Because stopping feels like work.

`[VISUAL: SPLIT. $10/MONTH on left. $120/YEAR on right. Annual figure rises slowly.]`

And now the gym's product starts to look different.

It is not simply access to equipment.

It is the monthly preservation of an intention.

The intention to get fit.

The intention to start next week.

The intention to become the kind of person who goes.

And the membership quietly bills you while that person does not arrive.

`[VISUAL: Cut from empty gym to modern software office.]`

The gym did not invent recurring revenue.

It simply revealed the psychological trick in its simplest form.

And then software applied the same logic to something much more valuable than a treadmill.

It applied it to ownership.

### ACT II — THE DAY ADOBE DELETED OWNERSHIP — 6:30

`[VISUAL: Boxed Photoshop CS6. Shrink-wrap. Slow three-quarter turn. Hold on the physical disc.]`

**NARRATION**

For about twenty years, buying software worked like buying an appliance.

You paid roughly seven hundred dollars for Photoshop.

A box arrived.

Inside was a disc and a licence key.

The important part was not the box.

It was the ending.

The payment happened once.

The tool remained usable.

`[VISUAL: Old retail box on left. Modern monthly subscription card on right.]`

From Adobe's perspective, that model had an awkward shape.

A new version shipped.

Revenue spiked.

People upgraded.

Then the line went quiet while perfectly functional copies stayed on people's computers.

`[VISUAL: Adobe revenue sawtooth.]`

That is an excellent product problem and a terrible recurring-revenue problem.

Because if the product is good enough, your customer has no reason to buy it again today.

**[OPEN LOOP]**

So what do you do when your best product becomes too good at being a one-time purchase?

On May 6, 2013, Adobe answered.

`[VISUAL: 2013 date. Adobe MAX conference.]`

Creative Suite was finished.

There would be no CS7.

The forward path was Creative Cloud — a subscription that continued as long as you paid.

`[VISUAL: BUY fades. SUBSCRIBE remains.]`

The backlash was real.

Petitions appeared.

Designers argued that under the old model, if they stopped paying Adobe, they still had the software they bought.

Under the new model, access depended on the subscription continuing.

`[VISUAL: Forum posts, tech coverage, petition screenshot, designer hands.]`

Adobe did it anyway.

`[BEAT — 0.7s silence.]`

Now watch the graph.

`[VISUAL: Revenue chart resumes. Sawtooth transforms into smoother recurring curve.]`

Adobe's subscription revenue in 2013 was about one point two three billion dollars.

`[BEAT.]`

By 2023 it was eighteen point two eight billion.

By 2025, total subscription revenue was about twenty-two point nine billion.

`[VISUAL: 2013 → 2023 → 2025. Three number cards.]`

The argument here is not that Photoshop became nineteen times better.

The software improved.

The economic relationship changed even more.

A customer who might return became a customer who had to keep paying to retain access.

That is a different machine.

And here's the uncomfortable lesson the rest of the market could see.

The customers were angry.

They subscribed anyway.

`[VISUAL: Anger headlines on left. Subscription revenue rising on right.]`

That meant the backlash was not the stopping force people imagined it was.

The recurring payment was stronger.

And if that could work for software, there was no reason it could not work for entertainment.

### STREAMING — 10:55

`[VISUAL: 2007 cable bill. Hard cut to early streaming interface. Then one modern TV screen with multiple service tiles.]`

**NARRATION**

This is where the story should have ended differently.

Streaming was supposed to kill the subscription model everyone hated.

Cable was the bundle.

One huge monthly payment.

Hundreds of channels.

You watched a fraction of them.

Streaming was supposed to be the escape.

Pay for what you want.

Cancel any time.

No installer.

No giant bundle.

`[VISUAL: One cable bill collapses into a clean streaming card.]`

And for a while, it worked.

Then every studio realised something.

Licensing the catalogue to somebody else's service meant somebody else owned the customer relationship.

So the catalogue split.

The show moved.

Another service launched.

Another payment appeared.

`[VISUAL: Netflix → Hulu → Prime Video → Disney+ → Max → Paramount+ → Peacock → Apple TV+. One card at a time.]`

A household that had escaped one large bill could now carry five or six smaller ones.

The exact bundle varies, but the psychological structure stays the same.

One giant bill feels like a problem.

Six smaller bills feel like noise.

`[VISUAL: One CABLE card physically fractures into six smaller cards. Total area remains visually comparable.]`

The industry rebuilt the bundle by fragmenting it.

And that creates a strange effect.

Every individual increase can look too small to fight.

A few dollars here.

A few dollars there.

A service you keep because one show is on it.

Another because sports are on it.

Another because your family's watchlist is there.

The consumer rarely makes one giant decision to accept the whole bundle.

The bundle appears one small decision at a time.

`[VISUAL: Six recurring charges stacking into one monthly total.]`

That is the same psychology we saw in the gym.

The payment becomes normal before the customer evaluates the whole system.

And once the economy is built on that recurring relationship, one question becomes much more valuable than the original sale.

Not: will you buy?

**Will you leave?**

**[RETENTION RESET — MIDPOINT]**

Because the next story is where the psychology becomes almost impossible to miss.

A customer tries to cancel.

And the company has already designed the path.

### ACT III — THE ILIAD — 13:20

`[VISUAL: Amazon Prime enrollment page. Cursor near the bright primary action. Secondary path understated.]`

**NARRATION**

In 2023, the Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon.

The allegations were not about whether Prime was expensive.

They were about enrollment — and cancellation.

The FTC alleged that Amazon designed parts of its signup flow so consumers could be enrolled without clearly understanding what had happened.

`[VISUAL: Signup flow recreation. Large action versus subdued alternate path.]`

But the part people remembered was what happened next.

The customer tried to leave.

And leaving was not one decision.

It was a sequence.

`[VISUAL: Screen 1. Confirm. Screen 2. Benefits. Screen 3. Discount. Screen 4. Pause. Screen 5. Confirm again.]`

Are you sure?

Here is what you will lose.

Would you rather pause?

How about a discount?

Are you really certain?

At several points, according to the case record, the visual hierarchy made continuing the cancellation less obvious than staying enrolled.

The customer was still technically free to leave.

They just had to keep walking.

`[VISUAL: A maze made of UI cards. The EXIT remains visible but distant.]`

And internally, Amazon employees had a name for the flow.

`[VISUAL: BLACK. Single word.]`

**Iliad.**

`[BEAT — 2 seconds.]`

The Iliad is an epic about a war that lasted ten years.

The name matters because it describes the feeling the company was creating.

Not a locked door.

A long path to the door.

`[VISUAL: FTC case document. Source line.]`

On September 25, 2025, Amazon settled the FTC case for two point five billion dollars.

`[BEAT.]`

One billion was a civil penalty.

One point five billion was allocated for consumer refunds related to the alleged enrollment practices.

`[VISUAL: $1B + $1.5B = $2.5B.]`

Amazon was not the only giant facing this question.

In 2024, the Department of Justice sued Adobe over subscription cancellation practices.

The complaint alleged that an early termination fee could be buried where customers would not clearly see it before subscribing.

The fee could reach fifty percent of the remaining contract value.

`[VISUAL: Cancellation screen. Fee appears late. Highlight it only after the full UI is visible.]`

That means a customer could sign up for something that felt monthly and discover later that leaving was expensive.

Two giant companies.

Two different products.

One underlying question.

How much work should it take to stop paying?

`[VISUAL: AMAZON $2.5B beside ADOBE settlement figure. Clean, documentary treatment.]`

And if the answer is “too much,” eventually the government tries to write a rule.

Which brings us to the part of the story that sounds like it should solve everything.

It doesn't.

### ACT IV — THE RULE THAT GOT CANCELLED — 15:15

`[VISUAL: FTC building in Washington, D.C. Cut to a clean policy document on a dark desk.]`

**NARRATION**

The rule was called click-to-cancel.

The idea was almost aggressively simple.

If you can start a subscription online with a few clicks, you should be able to end it with a comparable amount of effort.

`[VISUAL: Signup path and cancel path side by side. Symmetry.]`

No phone call.

No retention maze.

No Iliad.

It was a symmetry rule.

Make the door in and the door out comparable.

`[VISUAL: Black. Signup 3 steps. Cancel 3 steps.]`

And then the twist.

The rule existed.

It was coming.

And then it was gone.

On July 8, 2025, the Eighth Circuit vacated the FTC's rule on procedural grounds.

`[VISUAL: Court opinion, first page. Slow push. PROCEDURE appears only after source page is visible.]`

The court did not declare deceptive subscription practices good.

It held that the FTC had not completed a required preliminary analysis of the rule's costs and benefits before finalising it.

In other words, the rule was not defeated by a competing business model.

It was defeated by the process used to write it.

`[VISUAL: BLACK. PROCEDURE.]`

So in 2026, there is no single federal click-to-cancel rule in force across the United States.

The FTC has begun the rulemaking process again.

States have their own laws.

And individual companies can still face enforcement actions.

But the nationwide symmetry rule is not sitting over every subscription checkout.

`[VISUAL: 2025 RULE → VACATED → 2026 NEW PROCESS.]`

And now the viewer should notice the uncomfortable implication.

The friction is not merely a design flaw.

In many cases, it can still be legal.

The six-page cancellation flow.

The extra confirmation.

The retention offer.

The subscription that is easy to start and harder to stop.

A company does not have to hide the exit completely.

It only has to make the exit slightly more expensive in time, attention or irritation than the customer expects.

`[VISUAL: Subscription page. The cancel option remains visible, but the cursor hesitates.]`

That is the mechanism.

Not a locked door.

A door you are allowed to open.

After enough effort that you start asking whether it is worth it.

### LANDING — 17:35

`[VISUAL: EXACT CALLBACK TO THE OPENING GYM SHOT. Same angle. Same treadmill. Same fluorescent tone. Hold.]`

**NARRATION**

Go back to where this started.

Twenty point eight million members.

Two thousand eight hundred and ninety-six clubs.

Hundreds of people can stand inside a location at once.

Millions more can be paying customers who are somewhere else.

`[VISUAL: Empty room. No camera move.]`

The first time we saw this room, it looked like missing demand.

Now it looks like the business model.

`[BEAT — 1.0 second.]`

For most of economic history, a company made money by giving you something.

If it stopped delivering, you stopped paying.

The transaction had a natural ending.

The subscription changed that ending.

The payment continues automatically.

And it continues until you deliberately interrupt it.

`[VISUAL: Bank statement. Same recurring charge from earlier returns.]`

That is why the hundred-and-thirty-three-dollar gap is useful.

It is not evidence that every consumer wastes exactly $133.

It is the measured difference between what a group of consumers thought their recurring charges were and what those charges added up to when itemized.

The larger lesson is the one the whole video has been building toward.

`[VISUAL: $86 → $219 → $133. Hold.]`

The subscription economy does not need you to buy more every morning.

It needs you to stop noticing what you already bought.

That is a much cheaper machine.

And it works because human attention is finite.

We notice the new purchase.

We stop noticing the old one.

The new purchase feels like a decision.

The recurring payment feels like a background process.

`[VISUAL: Bank statement becomes a long vertical list. New charges glow; old charges fade.]`

So here's the only useful thing to do with any of this.

Open your banking app.

Search the last twelve months — not one month, twelve.

Look for charges that repeat around the same amount or date.

Every one of those was a decision you made once and have been renewing silently ever since.

Some you will want to keep.

Good.

Keeping it is a choice.

`[VISUAL: Cursor moves through recurring charges. It stops on the final one.]`

The point is that it should remain a choice.

Not a forgotten decision that keeps making itself.

`[VISUAL: CUT TO BLACK. 1.5 seconds.]`

**NO OUTRO. NO ENDSCREEN NARRATION. HARD STOP.**
