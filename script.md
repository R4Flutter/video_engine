# THE COMPANY THAT SELLS YOU NOTHING — LONG-FORM MASTER SCRIPT v2

**Topic:** Planet Fitness, subscriptions, recurring revenue, cancellation friction, and the economics of products people keep paying for without actively using.
**Format:** 16:9 YouTube long-form business documentary
**Target runtime:** 19:14
**Audience:** viewers who enjoy business stories, consumer psychology, and explainers with a strong narrative mystery
**Editorial mode:** investigative documentary — human, visual, evidence-led, not lecture-led
**Primary story question:** **Why can a company make more money from customers who barely use what they bought?**
**Secondary question:** **What changed when businesses learned that the hardest part of a subscription is not getting in — it is getting out?**

## NON-NEGOTIABLE STORY DESIGN

This is not a compressed Short stretched to nineteen minutes. The story is built in escalating chapters:

1. Strange observation: twenty million-plus members, an empty gym.
2. Mechanism: unused capacity is economically useful.
3. Name the mechanism: breakage and recurring revenue.
4. Old model: hard contracts created obvious victims and obvious complaints.
5. New model: low price plus recurring billing turns friction into the weapon.
6. Ownership reset: Adobe turns software from a thing you buy into access you keep paying for.
7. Subscription fragmentation: streaming recreates the bundle through many smaller charges.
8. Exit problem: Amazon and Adobe make cancellation friction a legal question.
9. Regulatory reversal: Click-to-Cancel appears, then is vacated on procedural grounds.
10. Callback: the empty gym is no longer an empty room — it is the business model.

### Retention architecture

Every chapter must do at least one of four things:

- answer the current question,
- create a more dangerous question,
- change the viewer's interpretation of what they already saw,
- or introduce a new character/company that raises the stakes.

Never spend more than one visual paragraph explaining a mechanism without changing scale, evidence type, character, location, or narrative question.

Every major section has a **mini-hook**, a **proof beat**, and a **forward question**. The viewer should feel that every answer has made the problem stranger.

# CHAPTER 1 — THE EMPTY GYM

## BEAT 1 — COLD OPEN / STRANGE OBSERVATION (0:00–0:20)

**Audio:**
"Planet Fitness ended 2025 with about twenty point eight million members. It had two thousand eight hundred and ninety-six clubs."

**Direction:** Start on the exact empty-gym composition. No logo montage. No title card. No intro music build. Fluorescent hum and room tone. Hold longer than feels comfortable. Then put **20.8 MILLION MEMBERS** over the empty room.

**Narrative job:** The image and the number contradict each other before we explain why.

**Hook:** THE COMPANY THAT SELLS YOU NOTHING

## BEAT 2 — THE IMPOSSIBLE RATIO (0:20–0:40)

**Audio:**
"That is roughly seven thousand two hundred members for every location. Four percent of those members would be about eight hundred and thirty thousand people. And that is the point. The business is not built for everybody to show up. It is built around the opposite possibility."

**Direction:** Count the ratio visually. Do not animate every number at once. Empty room → club count → 7,200 → four percent → one tiny user. The reveal lands only after the viewer has seen the mismatch.

## BEAT 3 — THE PERFECT CUSTOMER (0:40–1:00)

**Audio:**
"Because the member who never walks through this door is not necessarily a failed customer. They may be the perfect one. The trick is not the contract. It is the friction."

**Direction:** Empty treadmill cuts to a recurring bank charge. The transition should feel like the room itself has become a financial statement.

**Forward question:** Why would friction be useful to the company?

## BEAT 4 — THE $86 / $219 GAP (1:00–1:35)

**Audio:**
"Here's the number that makes the gym make sense. When researchers asked a thousand Americans to estimate their monthly subscription spending, the average answer was eighty-six dollars. Then the same people were forced to itemize the charges: streaming, music, cloud storage, the gym, the app downloaded for a trip two years ago. The average was two hundred and nineteen. One hundred and thirty-three dollars a month higher than people thought. A hundred and thirty-three dollars is not a rounding error. It is sixteen hundred dollars a year."

**Direction:** $86 must appear alone. Black hold. Then $219. Then isolate $133. Only after a pause convert it to roughly $1,600 per year. Keep the source qualifier visibly subordinate to the main claim.

**Forward question:** How does a payment become background noise?

## BEAT 5 — THE PATTERN SPREADS (1:35–2:05)

**Audio:**
"And the reason this matters is not that people are bad at arithmetic. It is that recurring payments are designed to become background noise. That gap is where the business model starts. Once one company discovered it could get paid for a service a customer barely used, the idea was portable. Another industry copied it. Then another. This is not one company's trick. It is a pattern."

**Direction:** Repeat the recurring charge until the viewer stops reading each individual line. Then abruptly clear the screen.

# CHAPTER 2 — WHY EMPTY CAPACITY IS PROFITABLE

## BEAT 6 — GYM ECONOMICS (2:05–2:40)

**Audio:**
"Start with the gym, because the gym makes the economics visible without hiding behind software. A gym has a strange cost structure. The building costs the same whether one person walks in or a thousand do. The machines cost the same. The lease, the lights, the insurance, the staff at the desk — most of it exists whether the customer arrives tonight or not. So an extra member can be extraordinarily cheap — until that member actually appears. Then they need a machine. A shower. Floor space. Staff time. The economics flip the instant the customer consumes the thing they bought."

**Direction:** Use a clean causal graphic, not a generic bar chart. Fixed costs stay mostly flat. Cost-to-serve stays low until capacity is actually consumed, then jumps.

## BEAT 7 — THE MEMBER WHO DOESN'T CONSUME (2:40–3:10)

**Audio:**
"So the ideal customer is not mysterious. It is the customer who pays — and does not consume. Industry estimates have put the share of gym memberships that go essentially unused at up to two-thirds. That is an industry estimate, not an audited universal number, but the direction matters. People intend to go. Most people intended to go in January."

**Direction:** January calendar → search interest / intention → February inactivity → charge continues. Treat the inactivity as human behavior, not as a joke.

## BEAT 8 — BREAKAGE (3:10–3:35)

**Audio:**
"There is a word for revenue a company collects for a service it never has to deliver. Breakage. The word came out of the gift-card business."

**Direction:** Everything disappears except **BREAKAGE**. Stamp sound. Hold. Let the word become memorable before continuing.

## BEAT 9 — ONE TIME VS EVERY MONTH (3:35–4:10)

**Audio:**
"A card gets bought, partly spent, thrown in a drawer, and the unused balance eventually becomes revenue that cost almost nothing to fulfill. Airlines have versions of it with expired miles. Retailers have it with store credit. But a gift card breaks once. A subscription is different. It can break again next month. And the month after that. And the month after that — until somebody actively interrupts the payment."

**Direction:** One-time breakage gets one calendar. Subscription breakage gets an endless calendar. That visual distinction is the point.

## BEAT 10 — FORGETTING AS REVENUE (4:10–4:50)

**Audio:**
"That changes the value of forgetting. For a subscription company, forgetting is not a one-time accident. It is recurring revenue."

**Direction:** The same charge continues down a long statement. Older instances fade into noise. End on a blank field before the next question appears.

**Forward question:** If this is more efficient than the old contract, why did older gyms use hard contracts?

# CHAPTER 3 — THE OLD TRAP HAD A WEAKNESS

## BEAT 11 — BALLY / THE OLD MODEL (4:50–5:35)

**Audio:**
"The first companies to industrialise this got it wrong, and the way they got it wrong is instructive. Through the nineties, the dominant American gym chain was Bally. Its model leaned on long contracts and hard cancellation terms. Customers described short-term offers turning into much longer obligations, billing disputes, and cancellations that did not behave the way the customer expected. Federal and state regulators investigated aspects of the company's billing and cancellation practices at different points in the 1990s and 2000s. Bally settled those matters and agreed to reforms."

**Direction:** Photographic archival evidence. Contract close-up → complaint form → regulator document. Do not dramatize claims not visible in the evidence.

## BEAT 12 — WRONG WEAPON (5:35–6:30)

**Audio:**
"Now read what happened next as a product-design lesson. Bally's model extracted money from customers who had stopped attending. But it also created something dangerous. It created a victim with a document. A customer could point at the contract and say: I tried to leave. That produces complaints. Complaints produce attorneys general. Attorneys general produce settlements. So the next generation removed the weapon that created the complaints: the obvious contract."

**On-screen:** THE CONTRACT WAS THE WRONG WEAPON

**Direction:** Build a causal chain on screen. Do not rush it. Each arrow should land on the next actor: customer → complaint → regulator → settlement.

**Forward question:** If the contract disappears, what replaces it?

# CHAPTER 4 — FRICTION BECOMES THE PRODUCT

## BEAT 13 — PLANET FITNESS / FRICTION (6:30–7:00)

**Audio:**
"Planet Fitness will let you cancel. There is no three-year term sitting on the screen waiting to be escaped. And that is exactly why the business model gets more interesting. You do not need a three-year trap if leaving costs twenty irritated minutes."

**Direction:** Reconstructed enrollment / account flow. Show a plausible sequence without falsely presenting generated UI as an official screenshot. Cursor movement must be human and slightly hesitant.

## BEAT 14 — $10 VS $99 (7:00–7:40)

**Audio:**
"And there is an even smarter piece. If you want to maximise breakage, a very high price can be the wrong tool. Charge ninety-nine dollars a month and the customer notices. It appears on the statement like a problem. Charge ten dollars and the psychology changes. Ten dollars is not free. But cancelling it can require a login, a password reset, a retention screen, and the irritating question: why are you leaving? Twenty minutes of friction to recover ten dollars."

**Direction:** $99 should look like a problem. $10 should look like background noise. Then reveal the hidden cost: time and attention.

## BEAT 15 — THE INTENTION PRODUCT (7:40–8:15)

**Audio:**
"Most people do the same mental calculation without writing it down. They keep paying. Not because they cannot afford to stop. Because stopping feels like work. The gym is not selling you fitness. It is selling the intention to become the person who goes. And then it bills you for the gap between that intention and your Tuesday evening."

**Direction:** Return to the empty gym. This time the audience should understand the image differently.

**Forward question:** What happens when software applies the same model to ownership?

# CHAPTER 5 — ADOBE: WHEN OWNERSHIP BECAME ACCESS

## BEAT 16 — ADOBE RESET / OWNERSHIP (8:15–8:55)

**Audio:**
"The gym did not invent the subscription trap. It industrialized the easiest version of it. And then software discovered it could do the same thing to something far more valuable than a treadmill: something you already owned. For about twenty years, buying software worked like buying an appliance: you bought the thing, then the transaction was over. You paid Adobe roughly seven hundred dollars for Photoshop. A box arrived. Inside was a disc and a licence key. The important part was not the box. It was the ending. The payment happened once, and the tool stayed with you."

**Direction:** Physical box, disc, key, shelf. Give the old world tactile weight. No motion until the audience understands the ownership model.

## BEAT 17 — ADOBE REVENUE PROBLEM (8:55–9:35)

**Audio:**
"The problem, from Adobe's side, was the revenue chart. A new version shipped. Revenue spiked. People upgraded. Then the line flattened again while working copies stayed perfectly usable. By 2012, the software was good enough that the customer did not have to feel urgency every eighteen months. Your product can be excellent and your revenue can still wait."

**Direction:** Sawtooth chart. Each spike gets a product release label. The line then stalls while the visual clock keeps moving.

## BEAT 18 — ADOBE DECISION (9:35–10:05)

**Audio:**
"On May 6, 2013, Adobe announced that Creative Suite was finished. There would be no CS7. The forward path was Creative Cloud: a monthly subscription that continued as long as you kept paying."

**Direction:** Date first. Then physical box. Then the words BUY fade away and SUBSCRIBE remains.

## BEAT 19 — BACKLASH / THE CHOICE HOLDS (10:05–10:40)

**Audio:**
"The reaction was furious. Petitions appeared. Professional designers argued that under the old model, if you stopped paying, you kept the tool. Under the new model, if you stopped paying, the software stopped being yours to use. Adobe did it anyway."

**Direction:** Use real-looking archival/forum evidence where available. Contrast the emotional backlash with the calm inevitability of the corporate decision.

## BEAT 20 — ADOBE NUMBERS / THE ECONOMIC MACHINE (10:40–11:00)

**Audio:**
"Adobe's subscription revenue in 2013 was about one point two three billion dollars. By 2023 it was eighteen point two eight billion. By 2025, Adobe's total subscription revenue had reached about twenty-two point nine billion. The point is not that Photoshop became eighteen times better. It did not. The billing relationship changed more dramatically than the product did. A customer who might buy again became a customer who had to keep paying to retain access."

**Direction:** One number per screen. Let each number settle before the next. After ~$22.9B, kill all graphics and let the narration finish over a quiet product image.

**Forward question:** What other industry could rebuild itself this way?

# CHAPTER 6 — THE NEW BUNDLE

## BEAT 21 — STREAMING RESET (11:00–11:25)

**Audio:**
"The clearest proof that the model spread is not in software. It is in the thing that was supposed to destroy cable."

**Direction:** Hard contrast cut: old cable bill → early streaming UI → modern TV with many services.

## BEAT 22 — CABLE VS STREAMING PROMISE (11:25–11:55)

**Audio:**
"For years, the argument against cable was the bundle. You paid one big monthly bill for hundreds of channels, watched a fraction of them, and had almost no way to buy exactly what you wanted. Streaming was sold as the escape. Pay for what you want. Cancel any time. No installer. No box. No giant cable package."

**Direction:** One promise at a time. Make the old cable bundle feel visually heavy, then make the early streaming proposition feel light and clean.

## BEAT 23 — THE CATALOGUE SPLITS (11:55–12:25)

**Audio:**
"And for a while, it was. Then every studio realised that licensing its catalogue to someone else's platform could be worth less than owning the subscription relationship itself. The catalogue split. The show you wanted moved. Another service launched. Another payment appeared."

**Direction:** Service cards multiply only when a sentence names another payment. Never do a logo parade for decoration.

## BEAT 24 — THE NEW BUNDLE (12:25–12:55)

**Audio:**
"By the middle of the decade, the average household was carrying multiple streaming services at once. The exact bundle varies, but the structure is familiar: several smaller recurring charges replacing one large recurring charge. The industry rebuilt the bundle. Same monthly recurrence. Same habit. The difference is that the bill is fragmented. One large bill is something you fight. Six smaller bills are easier to ignore."

**Direction:** One CABLE card fractures into smaller cards while the total monthly burden remains conceptually present. This is a structural analogy, not a claim about one exact household bundle.

## BEAT 25 — THE EXIT QUESTION (12:55–13:20)

**Audio:**
"That is why the streaming story matters here. The subscription did not merely sell access. It changed the psychology of the bill. And once everything is a subscription, product quality is only half the game. The other half is what happens at the exit. Because recurring revenue is strongest when the payment is automatic — and weakest when the customer starts thinking about stopping."

**Direction:** Move from many cards toward one cursor approaching a cancel button. Remove distractions as the exit becomes the only important thing in frame.

# CHAPTER 7 — THE EXIT BECOMES A LEGAL QUESTION

## BEAT 26 — AMAZON / ENROLLMENT (13:20–13:45)

**Audio:**
"In 2023, the Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon. The allegations were not about the price of Prime. They were about enrollment and cancellation. The FTC alleged that Amazon designed parts of the Prime signup process so consumers could be enrolled without meaningfully understanding what had happened. And then came the cancellation flow."

**Direction:** Recreated interface only. Label it clearly as a reconstruction whenever shown. Focus on the cursor, hierarchy, and decision path, not brand spectacle.

## BEAT 27 — THE CANCELLATION MAZE (13:45–14:10)

**Audio:**
"Each page asked whether you were sure. Each offered a reason to stay. At several points the continuation button was visually weaker than the button that abandoned the cancellation."

**Direction:** Build a sequence of increasingly annoying confirmations. Keep the physical camera movement slow while the interface steps accelerate. That contrast creates pressure.

## BEAT 28 — ILIAD (14:10–14:35)

**Audio:**
"And internally, according to the FTC case record, Amazon employees had a name for the sequence. Iliad. They named it after Homer's epic about a war that lasted ten years. The name is memorable because the mechanism is memorable. The customer is not physically trapped. They are procedurally delayed."

**Direction:** UI maze cuts to black. **ILIAD** appears alone. Then a physical book enters frame. Return to the UI from the book's shape.

## BEAT 29 — $2.5 BILLION (14:35–15:00)

**Audio:**
"On September 25, 2025, Amazon settled the FTC case. Two point five billion dollars. One billion was a civil penalty. One and a half billion was refunds to consumers harmed by the alleged Prime enrollment practices. Amazon also agreed to stop the practices the order covered."

**Direction:** Source document first. Then $2.5B alone. Then $1B + $1.5B. Do not show three numbers simultaneously.

## BEAT 30 — ADOBE CANCELLATION CASE (15:00–15:15)

**Audio:**
"Amazon was not alone. In 2024, the Department of Justice sued Adobe over subscription cancellation practices. The complaint alleged that an early termination fee could be buried where consumers would not see it clearly before subscribing. The fee could amount to fifty percent of the remaining contract value. In March 2026, the Justice Department announced a proposed one hundred and fifty million dollar resolution: seventy-five million in civil penalties and seventy-five million in free services for customers, plus new disclosure and cancellation protections."

**Direction:** Compress this section visually into evidence: complaint → fee field → settlement split. Do not turn it into a legal lecture.

## BEAT 31 — THE RULE SHOULD SOLVE IT (15:15–15:45)

**Audio:**
"Which raises the obvious question. If the penalties are this large, and the practice is this well documented, surely somebody finally wrote a rule. Somebody did. It was called Click-to-Cancel. The idea was almost aggressively simple: if you can start a subscription easily, you must be able to end it at least as easily. No phone call. No retention maze. No Iliad."

**Direction:** Present this as the apparent ending. Music lifts. Make the viewer feel that the problem has been solved.

## BEAT 32 — THE RULE IS GONE (15:45–16:15)

**Audio:**
"But companies and trade groups challenged the rule. And on July 8, 2025, the Eighth Circuit vacated the FTC's rule on procedural grounds. The rule existed. It was coming. And then it was gone."

**Direction:** Court opinion → date → RULE EXISTS → VACATED → GONE. One of the sharpest visual reversals in the film.

## BEAT 33 — PROCEDURE (16:15–16:45)

**Audio:**
"The court did not endorse deceptive subscription practices. It held that the FTC had failed to complete a required preliminary regulatory analysis of the rule's expected costs and benefits. Procedure killed the rule. And the timing is what makes it remarkable. The rule was vacated days before it was due to become enforceable."

**Direction:** Slow down. Highlight the operative procedural language. This is the section where precision matters more than spectacle.

## BEAT 34 — CURRENT STATE (16:45–17:15)

**Audio:**
"So in 2026, there is no federal Click-to-Cancel rule in effect in the United States. The FTC has started the rulemaking process again, and states have their own subscription laws, but the single nationwide rule is not in force. Which means the friction is still legal at the federal level in places where other laws do not prohibit it. The retention screens are legal. The six-page cancellation flow may still be legal. The plan that is easy to start on a phone and harder to end may still be legal."

**Direction:** Timeline: 2025 rule → vacated → 2026 process. Then a map of state variation. Keep the legal qualifier exact and visually prominent.

# CAMERA / EDITING LANGUAGE

Long-form camera motion is **motivated**, not decorative.

### Shot hierarchy

- **ESTABLISH:** locked or near-locked. Establishes a world before the camera starts searching.
- **DISCOVER:** slow push, micro-pan, or detail reveal that guides the eye toward newly relevant evidence.
- **THINK:** long hold or restrained pull-back during a conceptual sentence.
- **ESCALATE:** controlled push, lateral move, faster intercutting, or scale change when stakes rise.
- **REVERSE:** abrupt contrast cut or directional change when the story contradicts expectation.
- **RESOLVE:** settle or hold. Do not keep moving after the idea has landed.

### Motion budgets

- Do not animate every still.
- Do not use the same push distance repeatedly.
- Do not move foreground and background at the same speed when fake parallax is not visually justified.
- Prefer **one meaningful camera idea per shot**.
- Let important evidence become more stable, not more chaotic.
- During legal/evidence sections, reduce decorative motion and increase source legibility.
- During human-behavior sections, use physical action and natural camera inertia.

### Shot-duration logic

Shot duration is driven by information, not a global cuts-per-minute target.

- Hook / contradiction: 2–6 seconds per meaningful visual state.
- Explanatory mechanics: 3–8 seconds, with a purposeful change inside the shot when necessary.
- Human B-roll: 2–5 seconds, often cut on action.
- Documents / evidence: long enough to read the decisive phrase; never hide evidence underneath movement.
- Major reveal: 3–7 seconds of stable comprehension before the next beat.
- Final callback: longest average holds in the film.

### B-roll rule

Every B-roll shot must answer a noun or verb in the narration. Prefer:

- a cursor making a decision,
- a finger passing a recurring charge,
- a contract page turning to the cancellation section,
- a hand placing an old software box on a shelf,
- a calendar crossing a renewal date,
- a user hesitating before a cancel button,
- a machine sitting unused in an otherwise maintained gym.

Reject generic stock footage whose only purpose is "something business-looking."

### Sound / silence

The score should behave like a documentary score, not a Shorts bed.

- curiosity: sparse pulse / room tone
- mechanism: restrained movement
- contradiction: drop or sudden reduction
- major evidence: remove competing music for comprehension
- reversal: silence before the line lands
- callback: motif returns quietly
- final line: no CTA sting

Silence is an edit. Treat it as deliberately as a cut.

# RETENTION CHECKLIST BEFORE RENDER

- [ ] Frame zero contains the complete hook, not an animated promise that appears later.
- [ ] The empty gym is understood before it is explained.
- [ ] Every chapter introduces a new question before the previous answer goes stale.
- [ ] The midpoint changes industry: gym → software → streaming → regulation.
- [ ] The story has at least three major reversals: contract → friction, ownership → access, rule → vacated rule.
- [ ] The same visual idea is never repeated without changed meaning.
- [ ] No generic stock B-roll.
- [ ] Every claim that looks like evidence is paired with a source or a clear reconstruction label in the production asset plan.
- [ ] Camera movement is motivated by story state.
- [ ] Major evidence is readable before motion resumes.
- [ ] The final callback changes the meaning of the opening.
- [ ] No generic outro or subscribe CTA interrupts the payoff.

**Master principle:**

> **Make the viewer understand something, then make that understanding dangerous.**

The film should always be moving from **"that's interesting" → "wait, that's how it works?" → "oh, it happens everywhere" → "then what happens when I try to leave?" → "I need to check my own subscriptions."**
