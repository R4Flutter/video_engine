## 4. SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN — 0:00

`[VISUAL: EMPTY PLANET FITNESS AT 4:00 A.M. Wide locked-off shot. Fluorescent hum. Machines perfectly clean. Nobody there. Hold a full 5 seconds before voice.]`

**NARRATION**

Planet Fitness ended 2025 with about twenty point eight million members.

It had two thousand eight hundred and ninety-six clubs.

That is roughly seven thousand two hundred members for every location.

`[VISUAL: Membership number → club count → 7,200 ratio. Three hard cuts. No decorative animation.]`

Four percent of those members would be about eight hundred and thirty thousand people.

And that is the point.

The business is not built for everybody to show up.

It is built around the opposite possibility.

`[VISUAL: Cut back to the empty room. One person appears on one treadmill, tiny in frame.]`

Because the member who never walks through this door is not necessarily a failed customer.

They may be the perfect one.

`[BEAT — 0.8s silence.]`

The trick is not the contract.

It is the friction.

### THE STAKE — 0:40

`[VISUAL: Phone screen. A bank statement scrolling. Small recurring charges pass without attracting attention.]`

**NARRATION**

Here's the number that makes the gym make sense.

When researchers asked a thousand Americans to estimate their monthly subscription spending, the average answer was eighty-six dollars.

Then the same people were forced to itemize the charges: streaming, music, cloud storage, the gym, the app downloaded for a trip two years ago.

`[VISUAL: $86. Full black. Beat. Replace with $219.]`

The average was two hundred and nineteen.

One hundred and thirty-three dollars a month higher than people thought.

A hundred and thirty-three dollars is not a rounding error.

It is sixteen hundred dollars a year.

And the reason this matters is not that people are bad at arithmetic.

It is that recurring payments are designed to become background noise.

`[VISUAL: $10 charge disappears into a grid of other transactions. Cursor scrolls past it.]`

That gap is where the business model starts.

Once one company discovered it could get paid for a service a customer barely used, the idea was portable.

Another industry copied it.

Then another.

This is not one company's trick.

It is a pattern.

### ACT I — BREAKAGE — 1:35

`[VISUAL: Archival recreation — 1980s American health club. Aerobics, chrome machines, mirrored walls, VHS texture.]`

**NARRATION**

Start with the gym, because the gym makes the economics visible without hiding behind software.

A gym has a strange cost structure.

The building costs the same whether one person walks in or a thousand do.

The machines cost the same.

The lease, the lights, the insurance, the staff at the desk — most of it exists whether the customer arrives tonight or not.

`[VISUAL: Cost line sits flat. Revenue line rises with each additional member.]`

So an extra member can be extraordinarily cheap — until that member actually appears.

Then they need a machine.

A shower.

Floor space.

Staff time.

The economics flip the instant the customer consumes the thing they bought.

`[VISUAL: Second line labeled only in post: COST TO SERVE. Flat for a moment, then spikes at actual usage.]`

So the ideal customer is not mysterious.

It is the customer who pays — and does not consume.

Industry estimates have put the share of gym memberships that go essentially unused at up to two-thirds. That is an industry estimate, not an audited universal number, but the direction matters.

People intend to go.

Most people intended to go in January.

`[VISUAL: Google Trends-style recreation: gym search spike in January, collapse in February. Repeat the pattern across several years.]`

There is a word for revenue a company collects for a service it never has to deliver.

`[VISUAL: BLACK. Hold 1.2 seconds.]`

Breakage.

`[SFX: one dry paper click]`

The word came out of the gift-card business.

A card gets bought, partly spent, thrown in a drawer, and the unused balance eventually becomes revenue that cost almost nothing to fulfill.

Airlines have versions of it with expired miles.

Retailers have it with store credit.

But a gift card breaks once.

A subscription is different.

It can break again next month.

And the month after that.

And the month after that — until somebody actively interrupts the payment.

`[VISUAL: Calendar cards continue below the bottom of the frame. The charge repeats forever.]`

That changes the value of forgetting.

For a subscription company, forgetting is not a one-time accident.

It is recurring revenue.

### BALLY — 5:35

`[VISUAL: Archival recreation — Bally Total Fitness storefront, 1990s. Cut to membership desk with contracts.]`

**NARRATION**

The first companies to industrialise this got it wrong, and the way they got it wrong is instructive.

Through the nineties, the dominant American gym chain was Bally.

Its model leaned on long contracts and hard cancellation terms. Customers described short-term offers turning into much longer obligations, billing disputes, and cancellations that did not behave the way the customer expected.

`[VISUAL: Contract. Camera glides down the page and stops on the signature line, then continues into dense small print.]`

Federal and state regulators investigated aspects of the company's billing and cancellation practices at different points in the 1990s and 2000s.

Bally settled those matters and agreed to reforms.

`[VISUAL: Newspaper clips, consumer-affairs graphics, complaint forms. Dry treatment. No dramatic red.]`

Now read what happened next as a product-design lesson.

Bally's model extracted money from customers who had stopped attending.

But it also created something dangerous.

It created a victim with a document.

A customer could point at the contract and say: I tried to leave.

That produces complaints.

Complaints produce attorneys general.

Attorneys general produce settlements.

`[VISUAL: BLACK. White line: THE CONTRACT WAS THE WRONG WEAPON.]`

So the next generation removed the weapon that created the complaints: the obvious contract.

`[VISUAL: Planet Fitness storefront. Bright purple and yellow. Clean. Friendly. Almost aggressively ordinary.]`

Planet Fitness will let you cancel.

There is no three-year term sitting on the screen waiting to be escaped.

And that is exactly why the business model gets more interesting.

You do not need a three-year trap if leaving costs twenty irritated minutes.

`[VISUAL: generic account login → password reset → retention screen → confirmation. Twenty-minute implied journey.]`

And there is an even smarter piece.

If you want to maximise breakage, a very high price can be the wrong tool.

Charge ninety-nine dollars a month and the customer notices.

It appears on the statement like a problem.

Charge ten dollars and the psychology changes.

`[VISUAL: Bank statement. One $10 line item sits among dozens of similarly sized transactions.]`

Ten dollars is not free.

But cancelling it can require a login, a password reset, a retention screen, and the irritating question: why are you leaving?

Twenty minutes of friction to recover ten dollars.

Most people do the same mental calculation without writing it down.

They keep paying.

Not because they cannot afford to stop.

Because stopping feels like work.

`[VISUAL: SPLIT. $10/MONTH on left. $120/YEAR on right. The annual number appears only after the monthly one has held.]`

The gym is not selling you fitness.

It is selling the intention to become the person who goes.

And then it bills you for the gap between that intention and your Tuesday evening.

The gym did not invent the subscription trap.

It industrialized the easiest version of it.

And then software discovered it could do the same thing to something far more valuable than a treadmill: something you already owned.

### ACT II — THE DAY ADOBE DELETED OWNERSHIP — 6:30

`[VISUAL: Boxed Photoshop CS6. Shrink-wrap. Slow three-quarter product turn. Hold on the physical disc.]`

**NARRATION**

For about twenty years, buying software worked like buying an appliance: you bought the thing, then the transaction was over.

You paid Adobe roughly seven hundred dollars for Photoshop.

A box arrived.

Inside was a disc and a licence key.

The important part was not the box.

It was the ending.

The payment happened once, and the tool stayed with you.

`[VISUAL: Old retail box on left. Modern monthly subscription card on right. No logos yet.]`

The problem, from Adobe's side, was the revenue chart.

A new version shipped.

Revenue spiked.

People upgraded.

Then the line flattened again while working copies stayed perfectly usable.

`[VISUAL: Adobe revenue sawtooth — spike, decay, spike, decay.]`

By 2012, the software was good enough that the customer did not have to feel urgency every eighteen months.

That is an awkward position for a software company.

Your product can be excellent and your revenue can still wait.

`[VISUAL: Calendar pages advance. Revenue line stays flat.]`

On May 6, 2013, Adobe announced that Creative Suite was finished.

There would be no CS7.

The forward path was Creative Cloud: a monthly subscription that continued as long as you kept paying.

`[VISUAL: Hold on old purchase box. The word BUY fades. SUBSCRIBE remains.]`

The reaction was furious.

Petitions appeared.

Professional designers argued that under the old model, if you stopped paying, you kept the tool.

Under the new model, if you stopped paying, the software stopped being yours to use.

`[VISUAL: Rapid montage — forum posts, tech coverage, petition screenshot, designer hands.]`

Adobe did it anyway.

`[BEAT — 0.7s silence.]`

Here is the important part.

Adobe's subscription revenue in 2013 was about one point two three billion dollars.

`[BEAT.]`

By 2023 it was eighteen point two eight billion.

By 2025, Adobe's total subscription revenue had reached about twenty-two point nine billion.

`[VISUAL: 2013 → 2023 → 2025. Three number cards. No bar chart overload.]`

The point is not that Photoshop became eighteen times better.

It did not.

The billing relationship changed more dramatically than the product did.

A customer who might buy again became a customer who had to keep paying to retain access.

That is a different economic machine.

And once the machine worked in software, the rest of the consumer economy had no reason not to copy it.

### STREAMING — 11:00

`[VISUAL: 2007 cable bill. Hard cut to early streaming interface. Then one modern TV screen with multiple service tiles.]`

**NARRATION**

The clearest proof that the model spread is not in software.

It is in the thing that was supposed to destroy cable.

For years, the argument against cable was the bundle.

You paid one big monthly bill for hundreds of channels, watched a fraction of them, and had almost no way to buy exactly what you wanted.

Streaming was sold as the escape.

Pay for what you want.

Cancel any time.

No installer.

No box.

No giant cable package.

`[VISUAL: One cable bill collapses into a clean streaming card.]`

And for a while, it was.

Then every studio realised that licensing its catalogue to someone else's platform could be worth less than owning the subscription relationship itself.

The catalogue split.

The show you wanted moved.

Another service launched.

Another payment appeared.

`[VISUAL: Netflix → Hulu → Prime Video → Disney+ → Max → Paramount+ → Peacock → Apple TV+. One card per half-second.]`

By the middle of the decade, the average household was carrying multiple streaming services at once.

The exact bundle varies, but the structure is familiar: several smaller recurring charges replacing one large recurring charge.

The industry rebuilt the bundle.

Same monthly recurrence.

Same habit.

The difference is that the bill is fragmented.

One large bill is something you fight.

Six smaller bills are easier to ignore.

`[VISUAL: One CABLE card physically fractures into six smaller cards. Total area stays similar.]`

That is why the streaming story matters here.

The subscription did not merely sell access.

It changed the psychology of the bill.

And once everything is a subscription, product quality is only half the game.

The other half is what happens at the exit.

Because recurring revenue is strongest when the payment is automatic — and weakest when the customer starts thinking about stopping.

### ACT III — THE ILIAD — 13:30

`[VISUAL: Amazon Prime-style enrollment page. Cursor hovers over the bright action. A smaller secondary path sits beside it.]`

**NARRATION**

In 2023, the Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon.

The allegations were not about the price of Prime.

They were about enrollment and cancellation.

The FTC alleged that Amazon designed parts of the Prime signup process so consumers could be enrolled without meaningfully understanding what had happened.

And then came the cancellation flow.

`[VISUAL: Screen-by-screen reconstruction. Confirmation. Benefits. Pause. Discount. Confirmation again. The exit path gets longer.]`

Each page asked whether you were sure.

Each offered a reason to stay.

At several points the continuation button was visually weaker than the button that abandoned the cancellation.

And internally, according to the FTC case record, Amazon employees had a name for the sequence.

`[VISUAL: BLACK. Single word.]`

Iliad.

`[BEAT — 2 seconds.]`

They named it after Homer's epic about a war that lasted ten years.

The name is memorable because the mechanism is memorable.

The customer is not physically trapped.

They are procedurally delayed.

They can still leave.

The question is how much effort the company can make them spend first.

`[VISUAL: A maze made of UI cards. The final exit remains visible but far away.]`

On September 25, 2025, Amazon settled the FTC case.

`[BEAT.]`

Two point five billion dollars.

`[VISUAL: $2.5B appears alone. Black background. No music.]`

One billion was a civil penalty.

One and a half billion was refunds to consumers harmed by the alleged Prime enrollment practices.

Amazon also agreed to stop the practices the order covered.

`[VISUAL: $1B + $1.5B = $2.5B. Three cards, one equation.]`

Amazon was not alone.

In 2024, the Department of Justice sued Adobe over subscription cancellation practices.

The complaint alleged that an early termination fee could be buried where consumers would not see it clearly before subscribing.

The fee could amount to fifty percent of the remaining contract value.

`[VISUAL: Cancellation screen. Fee appears late, after several steps. Highlight the field in red.]`

In March 2026, the Justice Department announced a proposed $150 million settlement: $75 million in civil penalties and $75 million in free services for customers, plus new disclosure and cancellation protections.

`[VISUAL: AMAZON $2.5B beside ADOBE $150M. Weighted scale, not a flashy versus graphic.]`

Two giant companies.

Two different products.

The same underlying question.

How hard should it be to stop paying?

### ACT IV — THE RULE THAT GOT CANCELLED — 15:15

`[VISUAL: FTC building in Washington, D.C. Cut to a clean policy document on a dark desk.]`

**NARRATION**

Which raises the obvious question.

If the penalties are this large, and the practice is this well documented, surely somebody finally wrote a rule.

`[BEAT.]`

Somebody did.

It was called Click-to-Cancel.

The idea was almost aggressively simple: if you can start a subscription easily, you must be able to end it at least as easily.

`[VISUAL: Signup path and cancel path side by side. Three steps versus three steps.]`

No phone call.

No retention maze.

No Iliad.

It was not a radical product rule.

It was a symmetry rule.

Make the door in and the door out comparable.

But companies and trade groups challenged the rule.

And on July 8, 2025, the Eighth Circuit vacated the FTC's rule on procedural grounds.

`[VISUAL: Court opinion first page. Slow push. The word PROCEDURE appears only after the source page is visible.]`

The court did not endorse deceptive subscription practices.

It held that the FTC had failed to complete a required preliminary regulatory analysis of the rule's expected costs and benefits.

Procedure killed the rule.

`[VISUAL: BLACK. PROCEDURE.]`

And the timing is what makes it remarkable.

The rule was vacated days before it was due to become enforceable.

So in 2026, there is no federal Click-to-Cancel rule in effect in the United States.

The FTC has started the rulemaking process again, and states have their own subscription laws, but the single nationwide rule is not in force.

`[VISUAL: 2025 RULE → VACATED → 2026 NEW PROCESS.]`

Which means the friction is still legal at the federal level in places where other laws do not prohibit it.

The retention screens are legal.

The six-page cancellation flow may still be legal.

The plan that is easy to start on a phone and harder to end may still be legal.

Not necessarily because a regulator approves of the design.

But because the legal system has not finished writing the rule that would make the symmetry mandatory.

### LANDING — 17:35

`[VISUAL: EXACT CALLBACK TO THE OPENING GYM SHOT. Same angle. Same treadmill. Same fluorescent tone. Hold.]`

**NARRATION**

Go back to where this started.

Twenty point eight million members.

Two thousand eight hundred and ninety-six clubs.

Hundreds of people can stand inside a location at once; millions more can be paying customers who are somewhere else.

`[VISUAL: Empty room. No camera move.]`

For most of economic history, a company made money by giving you something.

If it stopped delivering, you stopped paying.

That link was the discipline of the transaction.

The subscription economy weakened that link.

The payment continues automatically.

And it keeps continuing until you take deliberate action to stop it — action the company has a financial reason to make slightly harder than you can be bothered with.

`[VISUAL: Bank statement. Cursor moves down the same list we saw earlier.]`

That is what the hundred-and-thirty-three-dollar gap is useful for seeing.

It is not evidence that every person is wasting exactly $133.

It is the measured gap between what a group of consumers thought their recurring charges were and what those charges added up to when itemized.

`[VISUAL: $86 → $219 → $133. The $133 remains.]`

So here is the only useful thing to do with any of this.

Open your banking app.

Search the last twelve months — not one month, twelve.

Look for charges that repeat at the same amount or around the same date.

Every one of those is a decision you made once and have been renewing silently ever since.

Some of them you will want to keep.

That's fine.

That is a choice.

`[VISUAL: Cursor stops on the final recurring charge.]`

The point is that it should be a choice.

Right now, for most people, it is just the last thing they agreed to — still running.

`[VISUAL: CUT TO BLACK. 1.5 seconds.]`

**NO OUTRO. NO ENDSCREEN NARRATION. HARD STOP.**
