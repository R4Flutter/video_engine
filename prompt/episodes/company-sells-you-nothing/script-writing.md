# Company Sells Nothing — High-Retention Script Writing System

This is the writing specification for the **Company Sells Nothing** documentary pipeline. It is deliberately derived from the existing `yt_engine` hook-learning work and the `new_story_script.md` example, then adapted to this project's documentary style.

The goal is **high engagement, high tension, and sustained curiosity without fake hype**.

---

## 1. Core principle

A strong script is not a list of facts.

It is a sequence of **questions the viewer feels compelled to answer**.

Every section should create a gap:

> **Claim → unanswered implication → evidence → reversal → new question**

Never resolve every interesting question immediately. Resolve one question while opening the next.

The `yt_engine` hook report found the strongest measured signals in its small corpus were **structural contradiction**, **open loops**, **shock + open loop**, and **curiosity before 5 seconds**. It also explicitly warns that the corpus was only 36 labeled hooks, so these are writing heuristics, not guarantees. cite-source-note

---

## 2. The first 5 seconds

The opening must establish three things quickly:

1. **A concrete subject** — company, product, law, number, event, or person.
2. **A contradiction** — something that should not logically be true.
3. **An unanswered question** — why the contradiction exists.

### Formula

> **Specific fact + impossible-looking implication + withheld explanation**

Example pattern:

> `[Company] has [huge number] customers.`
>
> `But if [small percentage] of them actually used the product at once, [unexpected consequence].`
>
> `That isn't a problem the company failed to solve. It's the business model.`

Do not begin with:

- "Today we're going to talk about..."
- "In this video..."
- "Have you ever wondered..."
- generic history
- channel introductions
- broad motivational statements
- empty adjectives such as "crazy", "insane", or "shocking"

The existing Company Sells Nothing script demonstrates this approach: it opens with Planet Fitness membership scale, gym capacity, the contradiction, and finally reframes the contradiction as the product. cite-source-note

## 3. Hook architecture

Use this sequence for the cold open:

### Beat A — Concrete reality

Give the viewer something measurable.

> "Planet Fitness has about eighteen point seven million members."

### Beat B — Scale comparison

Make the number meaningful.

> "It has roughly two thousand five hundred gyms."

### Beat C — Contradiction

Show why the numbers don't fit together.

> "If four percent showed up on the same evening, the fire marshal would close it."

### Beat D — Reframe

Do not explain everything. Give the first major insight.

> "This is not a risk the company manages. It's the product."

### Beat E — Open the larger story

Move from the specific example to the system behind it.

> "The gyms didn't invent this. They just made it visible."

The viewer should now be asking:

> **If this is intentional, how does the system work — and who else is doing it?**

---

## 4. The open-loop engine

An open loop is a question that the viewer consciously or subconsciously wants answered.

### Good loops

- "But that created a problem Adobe had not expected."
- "The contract was the wrong tool."
- "And the next company solved that problem in a completely different way."
- "There was one number inside the filing that changed the story."

### Weak loops

- "You'll never believe what happened next."
- "The answer will shock you."
- "But first, let's look at the history."
- "We'll get to that later."

A good loop contains **information**. A bad loop contains only **withholding**.

### Loop rule

Every major section should answer the previous section's main question while introducing a stronger question.

```text
QUESTION 1
   ↓
ANSWER 1
   ↓
BUT / HOWEVER
   ↓
QUESTION 2
   ↓
ANSWER 2
   ↓
REVEAL / REVERSAL
   ↓
QUESTION 3
```

---

## 5. The tension ladder

Do not keep every beat at maximum intensity. Build upward.

### Level 1 — Curiosity

"Why does this number look wrong?"

### Level 2 — Contradiction

"The business appears to benefit from the exact behavior it claims to discourage."

### Level 3 — Stakes

"This changes how billions of dollars move."

### Level 4 — Mechanism

"Here is exactly how the system produces that outcome."

### Level 5 — Reversal

"The obvious explanation is wrong."

### Level 6 — Consequence

"The same mechanism appears somewhere much larger."

### Level 7 — Personal landing

"Here is what the mechanism costs an ordinary person."

The strongest documentaries repeatedly climb this ladder instead of remaining on one emotional level.

---

## 6. Reversal writing

A reversal should change the viewer's interpretation of something they already understood.

Use:

> **What you think → evidence → but → what is actually happening**

Examples of structural reversals:

- The company does not need to trap customers; it only needs cancellation to feel inconvenient.
- The subscription is not primarily about delivering more service; it changes the timing and predictability of revenue.
- The problem was not that consumers disliked the new model; the problem was that they continued buying it anyway.
- The regulation arrived, but the business model had already adapted around it.

Avoid reversals that are merely dramatic wording. The underlying evidence must actually change the interpretation.

---

## 7. Section architecture

Build each major section using this six-beat structure:

### 1. New information

Introduce an entity, number, event, or mechanism.

### 2. Why it matters

Explain the consequence, not just the fact.

### 3. Evidence

Use a document, filing, quote, chart, archival image, contract, timeline, or concrete example.

### 4. Contradiction

Show what does not fit the obvious explanation.

### 5. Reversal

Reveal the deeper mechanism.

### 6. Bridge loop

End by making the next section necessary.

Example:

```text
Adobe sold software once.
↓
That produced irregular revenue.
↓
The revenue chart shows the problem.
↓
The software itself was already good enough.
↓
Adobe changed ownership into subscription access.
↓
But another industry had already discovered the same recurring-revenue trick.
```

## 8. The 30-second retention test

For the first 30 seconds, check every sentence:

### Sentence 1
Can the viewer picture it?

### Sentence 2
Does it make sentence 1 more surprising?

### Sentence 3
Does it introduce a contradiction?

### Sentence 4
Does the viewer now need an explanation?

### Sentence 5–8
Do we provide evidence without completely closing the loop?

### By ~20–30 seconds
Has the story expanded into a larger problem?

If the opening can be skipped without losing the viewer's understanding, rewrite it.

---

## 9. Numbers are story weapons

Do not dump statistics.

Use numbers to create a mental picture or contradiction.

### Weak
> "The company has 18.7 million members and 2,500 locations."

### Strong
> "18.7 million members. 2,500 gyms. If four percent arrived on the same night, the fire marshal would close the building."

The number becomes meaningful because it changes what the viewer thinks the business is selling.

### Number rhythm

For major figures:

> **Statement → pause → number → interpretation**

The existing Company Sells Nothing script specifically calls for a full beat of silence before major dollar figures and uses number spikes as retention devices. cite-source-note

---

## 10. Entity rotation

Do not spend too long explaining one company, product, or mechanism.

Introduce a new entity when curiosity begins to flatten.

Useful entity types:

- company
- customer
- competitor
- regulator
- law
- contract
- product
- historical event
- document
- number
- person

The existing script maps retention devices around new entities, reversals, and number spikes rather than relying on constant narration intensity. cite-source-note

A practical long-form rhythm is:

```text
ENTITY → MECHANISM → CONSEQUENCE → NEW ENTITY → REVERSAL → NEW QUESTION
```

---

## 11. The bridge sentence

The final sentence of a section should make the next section feel inevitable.

### Weak
> "Now let's talk about Adobe."

### Strong
> "The gyms didn't invent this. They just made it visible. Because at almost exactly the same moment, another company was applying the same mathematics to something you already owned."

The bridge should carry **causal momentum**, not just chronology.

---

## 12. Ending the loop

The ending should return to the opening question or image.

Use:

> **Opening contradiction → investigation → mechanism → wider consequence → original contradiction reinterpreted**

Do not introduce a completely new thesis in the final minute.

Do not add a long outro.

Do not say:

- "Thanks for watching"
- "Don't forget to subscribe"
- "Let me know what you think"

The final line should land the thesis and stop.

The existing script explicitly specifies a hard stop at the end rather than adding material after the landing. cite-source-note

---

## 13. Writing voice for Company Sells Nothing

The voice is **measured documentary analysis**, not YouTube hype.

Prefer:

- short declarative sentences
- concrete nouns
- exact numbers
- restrained skepticism
- causal explanations
- dry observations
- evidence before judgment

Avoid:

- "crazy"
- "insane"
- "you guys"
- "let's dive in"
- exaggerated adjectives
- fake urgency
- constant rhetorical questions
- moralising before establishing the mechanism

The current Company Sells Nothing script explicitly specifies a measured register and says the facts should carry the biggest claims rather than an exaggerated read. cite-source-note

## 14. Script-generation algorithm

When generating a new Company Sells Nothing story:

### Step 1 — Find the contradiction

Write one sentence:

> **"The strange thing about X is that _____."**

If this sentence is weak, the story is probably weak.

### Step 2 — Find the mechanism

Answer:

> **"How does the company actually make this work?"**

### Step 3 — Find the stakes

Answer:

> **"What changes if the viewer understands this mechanism?"**

### Step 4 — Find three reversals

For each major act:

> **"What does the viewer initially believe, and what evidence will force them to update that belief?"**

### Step 5 — Build the open loops

At the end of each act, write the next unanswered question.

### Step 6 — Place retention devices

Prioritize:

1. contradiction
2. open loop
3. new entity
4. reversal
5. number spike
6. consequence

The `yt_engine` report specifically identified structural contradiction and open loops among its strongest observed patterns, while emphasizing that the evidence base was still small and low-confidence. cite-source-note

### Step 7 — Write the cold open last

After understanding the whole story, choose the most powerful contradiction and build the first 30–60 seconds around it.

### Step 8 — Rewrite for compression

Delete:

- repeated context
- generic transitions
- obvious conclusions
- facts that do not change the viewer's understanding
- adjectives that evidence already communicates

### Step 9 — Audit every minute

Ask:

> **What new thing did the viewer learn?**
>
> **What question are they now waiting to have answered?**
>
> **Did the interpretation change?**

If the answer is "nothing" for a section, rewrite it.

## 15. Required script output format

Every generated documentary script should contain:

```text
TITLE / PACKAGE

CORE CONTRADICTION

COLD OPEN
- visual
- narration
- open loop

STAKE
- why this matters

ACT I
- entity
- mechanism
- evidence
- reversal
- bridge loop

ACT II
- entity
- mechanism
- evidence
- reversal
- bridge loop

ACT III
- entity
- mechanism
- evidence
- highest-stakes reversal
- consequence

LANDING
- answer to opening contradiction
- personal/systemic implication
- hard stop

RETENTION MAP
- opening hook
- open loops
- new entities
- reversals
- number spikes
- final reframe
```

## 16. Final quality gate

Reject the script if any of these are true:

- The first 10 seconds contain no concrete subject.
- There is no contradiction in the opening.
- The opening explains everything immediately.
- Sections are chronological summaries with no tension.
- New facts do not change the viewer's interpretation.
- Open loops are clickbait rather than real questions.
- The script relies on adjectives instead of evidence.
- Multiple sections repeat the same mechanism.
- The ending introduces a new topic.
- The final line does not reframe the opening.

### The target

The viewer should repeatedly feel:

> **"Wait — if that's true, then how does this work?"**

Then the script answers that question and immediately creates a more interesting one.

That is the core writing loop.
