# STORY ENGINE — PRODUCTION VIRALITY MODE

This is the editorial contract for long-form YouTube scripts. It sits BEFORE the visual director.

The objective is not to maximize "viral wording". The objective is to maximize the probability that a real viewer chooses the video, keeps watching, understands the promise, feels the story was worth the time, and reaches a satisfying payoff.

YouTube's own guidance frames performance around three connected outcomes: appeal (do viewers choose it), engagement (do they stay), and satisfaction (did they enjoy it). The script system therefore treats STORY as the primary product and editing as the delivery layer.

## 1. NON-NEGOTIABLE RULE

Never write a long-form script as a sequence of facts with transitions.

A production script must contain a narrative engine:

`PROMISE → MYSTERY → STAKES → ESCALATION → DISCOVERY → COMPLICATION → PROOF → REVERSAL → PAYOFF`

Not every episode needs every label literally, but the underlying functions must exist.

## 2. BEFORE WRITING THE SCRIPT

Build an internal story brief first.

Required fields:

- Core audience: who cares immediately and why
- Universal problem: why the topic matters beyond the niche
- Central question: the one question the viewer wants answered
- Story promise: what the viewer will understand by the end
- Stakes: what is lost, won, changed, or surprising
- Primary character / case / company / object when appropriate
- Counterforce: what makes the obvious answer wrong or incomplete
- Evidence ladder: the 3–7 strongest verified facts, events, examples, or demonstrations
- Final revelation: the idea that should make the whole story snap into focus
- Title promise: what curiosity the packaging creates
- Payoff proof: the exact evidence that satisfies the promise

If the central question can be answered in one sentence before the first major turn, the premise is probably too shallow. Strengthen the mystery before drafting.

## 3. HOOK ENGINE

Generate at least 8 distinct opening concepts before choosing one.

Required archetype coverage where applicable:

- outcome/mystery
- counterintuitive claim
- story cold-open
- high-stakes consequence
- comparison
- question
- strange fact
- time/place event

Score each candidate from 0–10 on:

- immediate clarity
- curiosity
- specificity
- stakes
- credibility
- novelty
- visual potential
- payoff potential
- title/thumbnail alignment
- honesty

Reject hooks that rely on vague clickbait, fake urgency, unsupported superlatives, or promises the episode cannot fulfill.

The selected hook must create a question that the next section actively advances.

## 4. OPEN LOOP DISCIPLINE

A curiosity loop is only valid if it is eventually paid off.

Track every open loop as:

`loop_id, opened_at, question, escalation, payoff_at`

Rules:

- Open a concrete question early.
- Do not open five unrelated questions at once.
- Every major loop must either resolve or intentionally transform into a larger question.
- Do not repeat the hook as fake suspense.
- Do not ask rhetorical questions without creating narrative movement.

The viewer should frequently know what they are waiting to learn next.

## 5. STORY MOVEMENTS

For long-form, design 4–7 movements rather than 12–20 disconnected informational beats.

A strong default:

1. Hook + impossible-to-ignore question
2. Setup + stakes
3. First explanation / apparent answer
4. Complication / contradiction
5. Evidence / case / experiment
6. Reversal / deeper mechanism
7. Final payoff + implication

Each movement must change the viewer's understanding.

If removing a movement would not change the story, remove it.

## 6. INFORMATION IS NOT PROGRESSION

A paragraph is not a beat simply because it contains a new fact.

Every beat must perform one of these jobs:

- reveal new information
- create a new question
- raise stakes
- overturn an assumption
- provide proof
- introduce a meaningful character/case change
- narrow the answer
- deliver a payoff

A beat that only explains already-understood material is a candidate for deletion.

## 7. CHARACTER / CASE SPINE

Whenever the subject supports it, anchor abstract information in a person, company, object, decision, experiment, or concrete case.

Instead of:

"Companies often fail because incentives are misaligned."

Prefer a story mechanism:

"The company had already built the answer. The problem was that the answer threatened the business that was paying the bills."

Use concrete cases to make abstract mechanisms emotionally legible.

If a topic truly cannot support a character, use a concrete before/after comparison or experiment as the spine.

## 8. ESCALATION LADDER

At least three meaningful escalations should occur in a substantive long-form episode.

Typical ladder:

`interesting → costly → surprising → consequential → irreversible`

Escalation must change stakes, not merely increase adjectives.

Bad:

"This was big. Then it got bigger. Then it got huge."

Good:

"At first it looked like a technical mistake. Then the same mistake changed the economics. Finally, fixing it would have destroyed the company's existing advantage."

## 9. MIDPOINT TURN

Do not let the first answer become the final answer.

A strong midpoint should introduce a complication such as:

- the obvious culprit was not the real culprit
- the supposed advantage became the weakness
- the solution created a second problem
- the winner was losing for a different reason
- a previously mentioned fact now means something different

This is one of the primary tools for preventing the middle from becoming a lecture.

## 10. EVIDENCE LADDER

Do not dump all evidence at once.

Sequence evidence so each proof unlocks the next question.

Preferred pattern:

`claim → first evidence → implication → second evidence → contradiction → stronger evidence → conclusion`

Separate FACT, EXAMPLE, ESTIMATE, and INTERPRETATION.

Never invent numbers, dates, quotes, market statistics, financial outcomes, or historical details.

For externally sourced facts, retain source metadata where the pipeline supports it.

## 11. NARRATION STYLE

Write for speech, not for a report.

Requirements:

- concrete verbs
- short-to-medium sentences
- varied sentence length
- occasional fragments for emphasis
- precise nouns
- minimal throat-clearing
- no repeated thesis statements
- no generic "but here's the thing" filler
- no paragraph that merely announces what the next paragraph will explain
- no fake conversational filler
- no inflated adjectives when a concrete detail is available

The voice should feel like a smart human telling a story to one person.

## 12. REVEAL ECONOMY

Do not spend a reveal before it has enough setup to matter.

For each major reveal, define:

- what the viewer believes before it
- what evidence arrives
- what changes
- why it matters

A reveal without a changed belief is decoration.

## 13. PAYOFF DESIGN

The payoff must answer the question created by the hook AND make earlier details feel more meaningful.

The best payoff does two things simultaneously:

1. resolves the central mystery
2. reframes the story

Do not end by repeating the thesis three different ways.

Deliver the strongest line once.

## 14. CTA RULE

The CTA is not allowed to hijack the emotional ending.

Preferred order:

`payoff → one sentence implication → short CTA`

The CTA should arise from the video's subject when possible.

Never add a generic subscribe paragraph after a finished emotional ending.

## 15. LONG-FORM PACING

Do not force identical beat lengths.

The narration, evidence density, and story movement should determine duration.

Every 20–45 seconds, verify that at least one of these changes:

- question
- stakes
- information level
- scene context
- narrative register
- evidence type
- character/case status

Long sections are allowed only when they are actively escalating or revealing.

## 16. VISUAL HANDOFF

Because visuals are generated separately, the script must expose visual intent without dictating a redundant visual style.

Every substantive beat should identify:

- what the viewer must understand
- what is newly revealed
- whether real-world B-roll, archive, evidence, chart, UI, subject, or graphic is required
- any hard negative such as `empty gym / no people`

The visual director chooses the medium; the story engine chooses the information.

## 17. ANTI-LECTURE TEST

After drafting, ask of every movement:

"Could this be turned into a textbook paragraph without losing the story?"

If yes, inject a concrete case, contradiction, decision, consequence, or evidence sequence.

## 18. ANTI-REPETITION TEST

For every repeated concept, label it:

`new evidence | new implication | new wording only`

Delete most `new wording only` repetitions.

A conclusion should not be repeated simply because the script has more runtime left.

## 19. VIRALITY GATE

A draft is NOT "production viral-ready" unless it passes all critical gates:

- title promise is concrete
- hook creates a specific unresolved question
- first minute contains meaningful progression
- at least one strong counterintuitive turn exists
- at least three escalations exist for substantive episodes
- evidence is progressive rather than dumped
- the middle contains a complication/reversal
- no major section is pure filler/exposition
- the final payoff resolves the opening question
- the payoff changes or deepens the viewer's understanding
- CTA does not weaken the ending
- no unsupported factual claims are presented as facts
- narration sounds natural when read aloud

A score is advisory. A critical gate failure is a production block.

## 20. SCORING MODEL

Score 0–10 for each:

1. premise strength
2. title promise alignment
3. hook
4. central mystery
5. stakes
6. character/case spine
7. escalation
8. midpoint turn
9. evidence quality
10. pacing
11. narration naturalness
12. curiosity continuity
13. reveal quality
14. payoff
15. emotional residue
16. visual handoff
17. factual integrity
18. CTA integration

Weighted overall:

- hook + premise: 15%
- mystery + curiosity continuity: 15%
- escalation + midpoint turn: 15%
- evidence + factuality: 15%
- pacing + narration: 15%
- payoff + emotional residue: 15%
- title promise alignment + visual handoff: 10%

Production thresholds:

`9.2+` = READY, assuming factual and asset gates pass
`8.5–9.19` = REVISE
`<8.5` = REBUILD

Never call a script "10/10 guaranteed viral." The system can enforce craft standards, not control audience behavior.

## 21. REWRITE LOOP

When the score is below READY:

1. identify the three largest losses
2. rewrite the premise or hook first if they are weak
3. rebuild the movement structure if curiosity continuity is weak
4. strengthen the case/character spine
5. add or replace the midpoint turn
6. remove repeated exposition
7. strengthen the final payoff
8. rescore

Never polish sentences before fixing story architecture.

## 22. GOLD STANDARD

The finished script should make a viewer say:

- "I need to know what the answer is."
- "Wait, that changes the story."
- "I didn't expect that."
- "Now I understand why that detail mattered."
- "That ending actually paid off the opening."

The system is successful when the viewer feels carried by a story, not processed through a content template.
